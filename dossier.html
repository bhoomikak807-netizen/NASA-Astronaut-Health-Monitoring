Research Concept

Navigation for spacecraft beyond the reach of GPS.

Technology Overview

Two physical references that do not depend on Earth.

QuantumNav Research explores quantum cold-atom inertial sensing and optical pulsar reference navigation for spacecraft operating in GPS-denied deep space.

A cold-atom interferometer measures acceleration and rotation by watching how clouds of laser-cooled atoms behave as matter waves. Because every atom of a given isotope is identical, the sensor has no mechanical parts to wear, creep or age, which is the main source of drift in conventional inertial units.

Pulsars give the absolute reference that inertial sensing alone cannot. Millisecond pulsars rotate with clock-like regularity. Comparing the arrival time of their pulses at the spacecraft against a timing model places the vehicle along each pulsar's line of sight.

Star tracker fixes provide attitude, so the inertial and pulsar measurements can be expressed in one inertial reference frame.

Inertial

Cold-atom interferometer

Measures 3D acceleration and angular velocity from the phase of atomic matter waves. Scale factor is set by the laser wavelength, a fixed physical constant.

Absolute position

Pulsar timing reference

Pulse arrival times from known millisecond pulsars bound accumulated position error along several independent sky directions.

Attitude

Star tracker

Matches observed star patterns to a catalog to give spacecraft orientation, aligning every sensor to one inertial frame.

Problem Addressed

GPS stops working long before the Moon, and Earth tracking is a shared queue.

Terrestrial GNSS signals from GPS and Galileo are aimed at Earth. Their main beams do not reach beyond geostationary orbit. A few missions have tracked weak sidelobe signals farther out, but accuracy drops quickly with distance and no usable service exists in deep space.

Beyond that boundary, spacecraft are navigated from the ground. NASA's Deep Space Network uses three antenna complexes about 120° apart in longitude so that at least one can always see a given spacecraft. Ranging and Doppler data come back to Earth, orbit solutions are computed by navigation teams, and corrections are sent up again.

This works, but it creates three bottlenecks:

  • Antenna time is scarce.Dozens of missions share the same dishes, and demand grows as more spacecraft head to the Moon and beyond.
  • Light-time delay.Round-trip signals to Mars take up to about 44 minutes, so the ground cannot react in real time to a trajectory error.
  • Single point of dependence.Navigation stops when contact is lost, during solar conjunction, or behind a planet or the Moon.

How It Works

Measuring motion with matter waves, then anchoring it to the sky.

The concept simulates atomic matter-wave interferometry to track 3D acceleration and angular velocity with near-zero sensor drift, enabling autonomous trajectory verification. One measurement cycle runs as follows.

  1. Cool and trapLaser cooling gathers about 10⁸ rubidium-87 atoms into a cloud at microkelvin temperature (Fig. 2).
  2. Release and splitThe trap switches off. A first laser pulse (π/2) puts every atom into a superposition of two momentum states, so each atom travels two paths at once.
  3. MirrorAfter time T a π pulse swaps the two momentum states, steering the paths back toward each other.
  4. Recombine and readAt 2T a final π/2 pulse closes the interferometer. The fraction of atoms in each output state reveals the phase shift Δφ.
  5. Convert and fuseΔφ is converted to acceleration and rotation, integrated into a trajectory, and corrected with star tracker and pulsar fixes.
Δφa = keff · a · T²
ΔφΩ = 2 keff · (Ω × v) · T²
keff is the effective wave number of the laser pulses (about 2 × 2π / 780 nm for counter-propagating Raman beams), a is acceleration, Ω is rotation rate, v is atom velocity and T is the time between pulses. Sensitivity grows with T², so longer free-fall times in microgravity are a natural advantage in space.

Telemetry Inputs and System Outputs

Three sensor streams in, one navigation solution out.

Telemetry inputs

  • Cold-Atom Interferometer Phase ShiftsRaw Δφ per sensing axis, converted to specific force and angular rate.
  • Star Tracker Optical FixesOrientation of the spacecraft body relative to the celestial frame.
  • Pulsar Timing ModelsEphemerides and spin parameters that predict pulse arrival times at any point in the solar system.

System outputs

  • Autonomous 3D State VectorPosition and velocity computed on board, with no ground-station round trip.
  • Drift-Free Dead Reckoning SolutionContinuous trajectory estimate whose error stays bounded by periodic absolute fixes.

Potential Applications

Where onboard navigation would matter most.

Deep space

Autonomous deep space trajectory control

A spacecraft that knows its own state can verify and correct its trajectory without waiting for a ground pass.

  • Time-critical events such as orbit insertion and flybys
  • Navigation through solar conjunction and communication blackouts
  • Less demand on shared Deep Space Network antenna time
Cislunar

GPS-denied cislunar navigation

Traffic between Earth and the Moon is growing, and most of that volume lies outside reliable GNSS coverage.

  • Transfers to lunar orbit and halo orbits around Earth–Moon Lagrange points
  • Operations on the lunar far side, out of direct view of Earth
  • A backup reference if ground tracking or relay links are unavailable

Research Status

Where the concept stands today.

Current maturity: Research Concept. All work to date is theoretical and simulation modeling only. No hardware has been built, tested in a relevant environment, or flown.

Evaluation relies on numerical simulation and on published external space research, including flight demonstrations by other organizations of pulsar-based navigation and of cold-atom physics in orbit.

Glossary and Background Reading

Terms used on this page.

GNSS
Global Navigation Satellite Systems such as GPS, Galileo, GLONASS and BeiDou.
DSN
NASA's Deep Space Network: antenna complexes at Goldstone, Madrid and Canberra.
Cold-atom interferometer (CAI)
A sensor that splits and recombines laser-cooled atoms as matter waves to measure inertial forces.
Millisecond pulsar
A rapidly rotating neutron star whose radio, X-ray or optical pulses arrive with very regular timing.
TOA
Time of arrival: when a pulse is observed, compared with when a timing model predicts it.
Dead reckoning
Estimating current position from a known start point plus measured motion over time.
State vector
The six numbers that describe a spacecraft's position and velocity in 3D.
TRL
Technology Readiness Level, a 1–9 scale from basic principles to flight-proven systems.
